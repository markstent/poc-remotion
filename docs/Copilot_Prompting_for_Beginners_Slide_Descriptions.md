# Copilot Prompting for Beginners

## Presentation overview

- **Source PowerPoint:** Copilot Prompting for Beginners (1).pptx
- **Main title:** Better prompts, better results
- **Subtitle:** How to brief Copilot like a smart new colleague
- **Length:** 25 slides, presented as a 25-minute workshop.
- **Audience:** Beginners using Microsoft 365 Copilot in a lottery and gaming business.
- **Format:** Facilitated workshop with live demonstrations, chat votes, two practical exercises, a cheat sheet and a closing task.
- **Main subjects:** Prompt construction using Goal, Context, Source and Expectations (GCSE), improving prompts, searching for work information and choosing a response mode.
- **Slide format:** Widescreen, 16:9.

## Design and presentation style

- **Main colours used:** Dark teal `#1D4757`, turquoise `#27E2CC`, lime `#C4F600`, pale blue `#CCFFFF` and white `#FFFFFF`.
- **Fonts specified in the PowerPoint:** Pangram Sans Medium, Pangram Sans Semibold and Pangram Sans Bold.
- **Typography:** Bold, generally left-aligned headings, small uppercase section labels and dark teal body text on light backgrounds. Dark teal slides use white headings and body text with turquoise or lime accents.
- **Layouts:** Split title and section slides, rounded information panels, before-and-after comparisons, tables, numbered exercises and horizontal prompt panels.
- **Imagery:** Repeated abstract artwork with lime, turquoise and translucent spheres. The opening slide also contains a small company logo.
- **Tables:** Four slides contain native tables, reproduced below as Markdown tables.
- **Presenter support:** All 25 slides contain speaker notes, including timings, talking points, demonstration instructions and likely questions.
- **Animation:** No built-in animation or slide-transition definitions were found in the PowerPoint.

## About this extraction

Each slide includes a description of its purpose and visual layout, followed by its on-slide text and speaker notes. The descriptions are written from inspection of the presentation. Source wording is preserved, with Markdown formatting added for readability. Automatic step numbering is represented as numbered lists. Slide-number placeholders in the notes are omitted. Product, news and pricing claims are transcribed as supplied and have not been independently verified.

## Slide 04: Four parts of a good prompt

### Slide description

Introduces the GCSE framework: Goal, Context, Source and Expectations. On a white background, a dark teal heading and explanatory sentence sit above four rounded panels arranged in a two-by-two grid. Each panel combines an oversized initial, the component name, a guiding question and a fragment of an example prompt. Goal uses turquoise, Context and Source use pale blue, and Expectations uses lime. Reading across the panels builds a more complete brief for Copilot.

### On-slide text

Four parts of a good prompt

Brief Copilot like a smart new colleague who knows nothing about your situation.

**G**

**Goal**

What do I want?

“Draft a short email about the launch delay…”

**C**

**Context**

Why, and for whom?

“…for retail partner managers, who need to replan shelf space…”

**S**

**Source**

What should Copilot use?

“…using the scratchcard launch plan and the print supplier’s email…”

**E**

**Expectations**

What should the answer look like?

“…under 150 words, three bullets, friendly but professional.”

### Speaker notes

**TIMING:** 0:03 · 1.5 minutes

**SAY**

- Brief Copilot like a smart new colleague who knows nothing about your situation
- Four parts: Goal, Context, Source, Expectations. GCSE, like the exams
- Same framework Microsoft teaches in its own guidance
- Context = the why and the background. Expectations = your constraints: length, format, tone
- Only Goal is required, which is why people stop there. The other three make it useful

**IF ASKED**

- "All four every time?" → No. Quick question: Goal is enough. Anything that matters: all four
- "Do I write the labels?" → No, normal sentences are fine
- "Does order matter?" → No
- "How long should it be?" → A few sentences. Detail beats brevity

## Slide 11: Six techniques to improve any prompt

### Slide description

A collection of six ways to refine a prompt beyond the GCSE framework. The white slide has a large dark teal heading and a short introductory sentence, followed by six pale blue rounded panels in two rows of three. Each panel contains a numbered technique heading and a short example or instruction. The equal-sized panels give similar visual weight to examples, stepwise work, reasoning instructions, role or tone, follow-up refinement and alternative versions.

### On-slide text

Six techniques to improve any prompt

GCSE already covers context and constraints. These add the rest.

**1 · Show an example**

“Write it in the same style as this email.” Paste it or point to it with /.

**2 · Break it into steps**

“First list the key points from these notes. Wait for me, then draft.”

**3 · Ask it to think first**

“Think through the options step by step before you recommend one.”

**4 · Give it a role or tone**

“Act as an experienced CRM marketer. Keep it warm and plain.”

**5 · Keep the chat going**

“Make it shorter.” “Now turn it into a table.” Expect to refine.

**6 · Ask for options**

“Give me 3 versions: formal, friendly and very short.”

### Speaker notes

**TIMING:** 0:11 · 1 minute, one line each

**SAY**

- GCSE already covers context and constraints. These six add the rest
- 1 Show an example: Copilot copies style and structure very reliably
- 2 Break it into steps: for big jobs, get the outline first, then the draft
- 3 Ask it to think first: "step by step" gives better reasoning on decisions
- 4 Role or tone: one line changes vocabulary, depth and voice
- 5 Keep the chat going: the first answer is a draft; expect to refine
- 6 Ask for options: useful when you're not sure what you want

**IF ASKED**

- "Does a role really change anything?" → Yes. Try with and without "Write it for a new retail partner" and compare
- "Can I paste an old email as the example?" → Yes, if it holds no personal data. Or point to it with /
- "Is 'think first' the same as Think deeper?" → Similar idea. The phrase works in any mode; Think deeper is the setting (Part 4)
